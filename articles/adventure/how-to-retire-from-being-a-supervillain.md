# How to Retire From Being a Supervillain

Congratulations. You have successfully completed your career as a supervillain.

You have an evil lair, a questionable empire, several hundred open issues, a henchman department, three backup command centers, and a GitHub organization that appears to own more infrastructure than you remember creating.

Unfortunately, retirement is not as simple as taking off your cape.

> **Warning:** Never leave active administrative access, important credentials, critical infrastructure, or ownership responsibilities behind just because you have dramatically announced your retirement.

## Things You'll Need

- A retirement plan
- A successor
- A complete inventory of evil projects
- A secure way to transfer legitimate ownership and access
- Documentation
- Backup copies of important records
- The legendary 47-page Supervillain Retirement Form
- A pen
- Approximately six hours
- Greg the plush monkey
- Seven rubber ducks
- One person whose entire job is saying, "Did you revoke that?"

## Step 1: Announce Your Retirement

Begin by making a formal announcement.

Stand on the balcony of your evil lair.

Put on the cape.

Activate the unnecessarily dramatic lighting.

Say:

> "I HAVE DECIDED TO RETIRE FROM SUPERVILLAINY."

Wait for the thunder.

If there is no thunder, play thunder sounds through the command center.

This is called maintaining standards.

Do not announce retirement before you have actually planned the transition.

Otherwise you will spend the next six months saying, "I'm retired," while continuing to approve pull requests at 2:00 AM.

## Step 2: Inventory Everything

Make a complete list of everything you own, operate, administer, maintain, or have somehow become responsible for.

Your list may include:

- Evil headquarters

- Git repositories

- Licenses
- Contracts

- The snack budget
- Greg's office
- The rubber-duck fleet
- The mysterious server labelled `DO NOT UNPLUG`

Do not assume something is unimportant because you forgot what it does.

That is exactly how the mysterious server became important.

## Step 3: Find Your Successor

Choose someone capable of taking over legitimate responsibilities.

Do not simply choose the person standing closest to the throne.

Ask:

- Do they understand the projects?
- Can they access the documentation?
- Do they know what is running?
- Do they understand the responsibilities involved?
- Do they have appropriate administrative access?
- Do they know what Greg is responsible for?

Your successor should not need to discover the entire empire through archaeology.

If there is no suitable successor, document the systems and establish an appropriate owner or maintenance plan instead of abandoning them.

## Step 5: Transfer Ownership Properly

Retirement does not mean disappearing while everyone else tries to figure out who owns everything.

Transfer legitimate ownership and responsibility deliberately.

For important software projects, document:

- Who owns the project
- Who maintains it
- Who can administer it
- Where the documentation lives
- Where backups live
- How incidents are handled
- What services depend on it
- What should happen if the successor becomes unavailable

For GitHub organizations specifically, ownership continuity matters. GitHub recommends having more than one organization owner so projects do not become inaccessible if a sole owner becomes unavailable. citeturn0search11turn0search1

Do not confuse "I gave someone the repository" with "the organization is now safely managed." Organization ownership, billing, repository access, teams, and other responsibilities may need separate attention. citeturn0search1turn0search2

## Step 6: Revoke Old Access

Once responsibilities have been transferred, review access.

Remove unnecessary administrative access.

Review:

- Organization membership
- Repository access
- Team membership
- Administrative roles
- Deployment credentials
- Service accounts
- Shared accounts
- API tokens
- SSH keys
- Automation identities
- Billing access

Access should match current responsibilities.

GitHub documents that removing organization membership can remove access to organization repositories, while local clones may remain with the former member. citeturn0search0turn0search4

Do not casually delete things during this process.

Retirement is a transition, not an excuse to press every red button in the building.

Do not start guessing passwords.

Do not run random commands.

Do not attempt to bypass authentication.

Instead, identify the system, use its legitimate account-recovery process, check your documented ownership records, and involve the appropriate administrator or provider.

Then document the account properly.

## Step 8: Shut Down What Actually Needs To Be Shut Down

Not everything needs to remain online forever.

Classify each system:

- Keep
- Transfer
- Archive
- Decommission
- Investigate
- **Absolutely Nobody Knows What This Does**

For anything you decommission, make sure you understand its dependencies first.

Back up what needs to be retained.

Record the change.

Confirm that nobody still depends on it.

Then shut it down using the normal, supported procedure.

Do not unplug random cables until something stops making noise.

That is not decommissioning.

That is archaeology with electricity.

## Common Mistakes

### Retiring Without Transferring Ownership

If nobody knows who is responsible for something, it is not retired.

It is abandoned.

### Leaving Administrative Access Behind

Former responsibilities should not require permanent administrative access.

Review access as part of the transition rather than six months later.

### Forgetting Billing

Subscriptions have no respect for villain retirement.

Review recurring costs and assign responsibility before leaving.

### Keeping Everything Running "Just In Case"

This is how EstroBunny acquired six evil lairs.

### Deleting Everything

Do not destroy useful history just because you are finished with it.

Archive deliberately.

### Creating One Last Repository

You have already created the retirement repository.

Stop.

## Emergency Procedure

If retirement has gone catastrophically wrong:

1. Stop making changes.
2. Identify what is currently running.
3. Identify who currently has administrative responsibility.
4. Preserve important evidence and documentation.
5. Restore legitimate access for the responsible team.
6. Recover accounts through supported recovery processes.
7. Check backups and recovery procedures.
8. Communicate clearly with everyone affected.
9. Document what happened.
10. Do not create another evil lair.
